Search is splitting into two jobs. One is the familiar competition for a place on a results page. The other is the competition to be the source an answer is assembled from, where there is no list to be fourth on.

## What is AEO, and how is it different from SEO?

Answer Engine Optimization is the work of getting a business quoted in the answers produced by AI systems such as ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google's AI overviews. SEO competes for a position on a results page. AEO competes to be the source an answer is built from, where there is no list and often only one mention.

The difference matters because the two behave differently under pressure. A search results page has ten positions and a user who scans several of them. An AI answer has one paragraph, sometimes with three citations underneath, and most people never open any of them. Being fourth in search still brings visitors. Being fourth in an answer engine brings nothing.

What changes in practice is smaller than the shift sounds. Answer engines read the same web everyone else does, so technical health, real content and credible mentions elsewhere still matter. What they reward more sharply is clarity. Content written as a direct answer to a specific question, in plain language, where the answer is complete in a single passage and does not depend on the rest of the page.

Two things carry more weight than they used to. The first is being described consistently across the web, because these systems assemble their understanding of a company from many sources and contradictions make it unreliable. The second is being genuinely specific. General claims cannot be cited. A concrete statement about how something works is quotable, and quotable is the whole game.

This page is built that way deliberately. Every question is answered in its first paragraph, completely, before anything else is said.

## How does a business get mentioned in ChatGPT and other AI answers?

By publishing clear answers to the questions buyers actually ask, being described the same way everywhere it appears online, and being mentioned by sources these systems already trust. There is no submission process and no way to buy placement. What can be influenced is how easy a company is to understand and to quote.

Answer engines build a picture of a company from everything they can find, then repeat what they conclude. So the work divides into two parts: being understandable, and being corroborated.

Understandable means the basic facts of the business are stated plainly and consistently. What you do, who you do it for, where you are, how long you have been doing it. When a company's own site, its directory listings and its profiles disagree about any of these, the system has to guess, and it will often guess wrong or say nothing at all.

Corroborated means being referenced somewhere other than your own website. A claim that appears only on your own pages is weak evidence. The same claim reflected in an industry publication, a client's site, a directory or a podcast is what turns it into something a system will repeat.

The content itself should be built to be lifted. A question as the heading, the answer immediately below it, complete in one passage, in language a non-specialist understands. Long preambles get skipped. Marketing language gets discounted.

What does not work is the tactic people reach for first, which is stuffing pages with variations of the question. These systems read for meaning, and repetition without substance reads as thin.

## Does local SEO matter for a company selling across Canada and the US?

Yes, more than most national companies expect. Buyers routinely add a city to a search when they want a supplier they can reach, and search engines localize results even when the query never mentions a place. A company in Toronto selling across North America still competes locally for a share of its own market.

Local search is usually explained with restaurants and dentists, which makes national businesses dismiss it. The mechanics apply more broadly than that.

Two things drive it. Buyers add geography when proximity implies something they want: faster response, easier site visits, a shared time zone, or simply a supplier they can go and see. In business-to-business purchasing that instinct is strong, particularly for anything involving installation, service or an ongoing relationship. And search engines localize results whether or not the query asked them to, so the same search run in Toronto and in Chicago returns different companies.

For a company in the Greater Toronto Area selling across Canada and the United States, that means two jobs rather than one. The local job is a properly maintained business profile, consistent address and phone details wherever they appear, and content that names the region without contorting itself to do it. The national job is being genuinely useful on the subject, which is what earns visibility where geography does not apply.

They reinforce each other. Local credibility is often the easiest place to establish authority, because the field is smaller. That authority then travels.
