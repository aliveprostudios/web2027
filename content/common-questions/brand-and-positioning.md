Brand work gets confused with design work constantly, and the confusion is expensive. The decisions below are made once and lived with for years, which is why they are worth slowing down for.

## What is brand positioning, and how is it different from a logo?

Positioning is the decision about what you are for, who you are for, and why someone should choose you over the alternative. A logo is one of the things that expresses it. Positioning is a strategic choice made once and defended for years. Identity is the work that makes that choice visible.

The confusion is understandable, because a rebrand usually arrives looking like a logo project. New mark, new colours, new website. Underneath, the question that decides whether any of it works was answered somewhere else, or never answered at all.

Positioning is subtraction. It is choosing which customers you are not for and which work you will not take, so the people you do want recognize themselves immediately. Companies find this uncomfortable, which is why so much brand language ends up describing everyone: quality, service, innovation, partnership. Those words fail not because they are untrue but because a competitor could sign the same sentence.

The test is simple. Read your positioning statement and ask whether a direct competitor could put their name on it without changing a word. If they could, it is a description rather than a position.

A logo cannot fix a positioning problem, and a good one will not hide it. What it can do is make a clear position unmistakable, consistently, everywhere the company shows up. That is a real job and worth doing well. It is just the second job.

The order matters because everything downstream inherits it: website copy, sales material, video, and the way the phone is answered.

## When does a business actually need to rebrand?

When the business has changed and the brand has not. New markets, a different kind of customer, a merger, a move upmarket, or a name that no longer describes what you sell. Boredom is not a reason. If the only argument is that leadership is tired of looking at it, the money is better spent elsewhere.

Rebrands fail most often when they are cosmetic answers to structural problems. Sales are down, so the brand gets refreshed, and six months later sales are still down because the issue was pricing, or the product, or a competitor who is simply better positioned.

The legitimate triggers have something in common: a gap has opened between what the company is and what it appears to be. A manufacturer that spent twenty years selling on price and now competes on engineering. A local firm that now serves three countries. A company whose name describes a product line it exited. In each case the brand is working against the business, and changing it is not vanity.

There is a quieter trigger worth naming. Some companies have never had a position at all. They grew on relationships and word of mouth, which worked until the founder stepped back or the market filled up. Nothing changed, and that is exactly the problem.

The cost people underestimate is not design. It is everything carrying the old brand: signage, packaging, catalogues, vehicles, uniforms, product literature, and the search equity attached to the old name. A rebrand is a business project with a design component, and treating it the other way round is how they run long and end badly.

## Why does our brand look different everywhere it shows up?

Because consistency is a process problem, not a design problem. Most inconsistent brands already have a style guide. What they lack is someone holding the line as work goes out under deadline, and a set of assets that makes the correct choice the easiest one for whoever is building the next thing.

Inconsistency accumulates. A sales deck is needed by Friday and someone rebuilds it from an old template. A trade show banner is ordered directly by a regional office. A supplier redraws the logo because they could not find the right file. None of these is a failure of care, and together they produce a company that looks like four companies.

Style guides rarely fix it, because a guide describes rules and the problem is enforcement under pressure. The person making Friday's deck is not going to read forty pages. They are going to find the closest existing file and change the words.

What works is making the correct path the fastest path. Real templates rather than examples. Assets in the formats people actually need, stored where they already look. A small number of decisions removed entirely, so there is no colour to get wrong. Then one person who sees the work before it goes out, with the standing to send it back.

The business cost is recognition. A brand becomes familiar through repetition, and every variation resets the count. A company that has shown up four slightly different ways for five years has spent five years buying recognition it never accumulated.
